The first time I really paid attention to "the devil's in the details" was back in high school, listening to Jordan Peterson lectures. I kept hearing it from him, in one form or another, and for whatever reason it stuck with me for years.

Then more recently I came across the other one. "God is in the details." I honestly can't remember where I saw it, but it caught me because of the contrast. It's a strange jump, going from the devil to God over the same handful of words. Same structure, same little observation about the small stuff, and yet one of them frames it as a threat and the other as something close to worship.

When I looked into it, it turned out the God version is the older one. The idea is that taking one's craft seriously can function as a form of worship, an idea I don't disagree with upon the condition that the details are taken care of with the **intention** of pleasing God. The devil version is the modern twist (who would've guessed?). It's more cynical, and it revolves one being weary of the steps they take, lest they leave the devil in the details and be caught in an evil trap later on. Now, this may just be my own biases, but the devil being in the details gives off this inspiration to conduct a deep cleanse and root out the devil from each and every crevice. An inspiration that hits everyone, because who wouldn't want to fight off evil? Whereas with God being in the details, it seems the action of dealing with the details is reserved for "the truly pious." If this impression holds up beyond my bias, it goes to show how much our innate disposition inclines us away from evil and how much our ignorance/arrogance prevent us from worshipping God.

When I wrote about Publix, that was the God side the whole way through. The green, the warm colors, the lower roof, the music playing softly, and the directions everywhere. None of it is necessary. You could run a store without any of it. But every one of those small things impacts how the place feels, and that's the point behind them. Then, a couple of days ago, I wrote about Murphy's law, and that was the devil side. The voice system transcribing Chinese while the user is speaking English was the single edge case I never would've thought to check, sitting there and waiting to ruin some users experience.

If the devil and God are both in the details, then the obvious move is to obsess over all of them. Obviously that's wrong, and I'll be the first to say it as someone that struggles with perfectionist tendencies.

The real question was never whether the details matter. It's **which ones**. Which details are load-bearing and which ones you can let slide. That's the crux of the game, and it's not easy...

I don't think I have a reliable way to spot the load-bearing detail. I don't think anyone does, at least not from inside their own head. You can't reason your way to it sitting at a desk. The way you actually find out is from reality. This is the entire premise of a book like The Mom Test. You don't trust your own read on what people want, because you'll fool yourself and others will fool you too, so you go and look at what they actually do. The significant details come from the data. In other words, reality tells you all that you need to know, you just gotta be listening (at the right frequency, I might add). You can't ignore reality.

The issue with this idea is that it only works when you can iterate on the thing in question. This is true for most things. For most of what we do, if we get it wrong, the cost is cheap (typically just time). We try something, reality gives us feedback, and we fix it. This is the thing about starting a business that took me a while to accept. You can't get everything right from the start, and trying to is a trap. You do some things, get through them, learn from the experience, keep the business alive, and improve as you go. The mistakes are non-fatal as long as you actually learn from them.

Then there's the other kind of detail. The kind with no second chance. These, quite reasonably so, are the ones that actually deserve the obsession, because by the time reality gives us feedback, it's already too late to act on it. On this point, one of the worst blunders that can be made is to treat a recoverable detail like an irreversible one, or worse, to get so attached to a single detail that you can't move past it. The best example of this blunder is in Albert Einstein. About a year ago, I read his biography (by Walter Isaacson), and he's basically the cautionary tale of this blunder. He had one detail he refused to let go of, that "God doesn't play dice with the universe", and he held onto it so hard that Quantum Physics moved on without him, even though he had been one of its pioneers. By the way, this relates to the famous interaction where Niels Bohr told Einstein to "Stop telling God what to do." One of the tuffest quotes out there 🥶.

Beyond the capacity to iterate, there's a second attribute that decides whether a detail is worth it. Reach. How many people touch it and how often. Around the same time I read Einsteins' biography, I read Steve Jobs' as well. He was famously a rough leader who demanded the absolute best from everyone around him, and people love to point at how he obsessed over the parts of the machine no customer would ever see. But I think that last part is overvalued. Simply, people don't care what's going on inside the phone as long as it works. However, Apple, and particularly Jobs, had a vision for how many people the phones can reach. And when a thing is touched by that many hands that many times, the math on a small detail completely changes. More small details begin to matter, and so, **consistency** in taking care of these details becomes important. Tying it all back, taking care of the details in that which people won't see is sure to contribute immensely to how the details are managed with regard to what people *will* see

As I was thinking of perfection, I came across the thought that it's only bad because of its dependence execution. I mean that the more perfectionist a person or system is, the less they will be able to execute on, because their execution capacity is hyper-focused on a few matters. While that idea does hold, it didn't feel right. With AI nowadays, people's execution capacity shot way up, and yet we're still seeing a surplus of low-quality work. Perfectionists can do more of what they're naturally inclined to do, but it's not producing better results... And then it clicked. Perfectionism boils down to direction AND execution. How the perfection, which should be clarified as attention to details, turns out depends on the direction you point it in (i.e. which details you pay attention to and which you leave for later). That part is still on you. Get the direction wrong and AI just helps you produce, beautifully and quickly, something that dies off as just another project.

There's something calming in that, weirdly. It means there's still a part of this that's ours to get right. And when I think about what that part actually is, at least in the business sense, it's understanding people and what they care about. That's the whole skill, and it's not an easy thing to do. Maybe it's just a sentimental thought, but I think there's something beautiful that when you follow all of this down to the bottom, the idea being able to understand other people.
