In the last piece, I made a case for looking at questions as challenges. I didn't address why that isn't already the default way of approaching things. That's what I want to do here.

But first, we must take a look at the terminology used. As per the title and the subsequent description, I make use of "think." But "thinking" wasn't a part of the original discussion.

The original discussion was about asking questions. So, it seems that it would be more appropriate for the title to be "why don't people ask questions?" I could just rewrite it, but I think there's a deeper connection to be made between thinking and asking questions.

When we think, it seems that our thoughts are nothing but responses to a series of implicit questions. When we are trying to think about something but can't, the issue lies in not knowing what the right questions to ask are (beyond just answering any particular question the wrong way). And to be clear, I'm referencing the state of thinking where there's actual effort being put in. "Conscious thinking" so to speak. I think I'll explore this more in a separate piece. Point is: it's not unjustified to reframe thinking as an internal question-answering process.

Now, the reason I was inspired to ask this question is that I noticed a discrepancy between the modern behavior of most people and the natural dispositions of children. I've thought a lot about how modern society shapes children into modern adults, and I think that'll be a source of topics for later pieces, inshaAllah. The particular discrepancy is based on the idea that, as children, we (id est humans) are predisposed to learning. And we primarily go about this through exploration.

When we craft the environment to be about some particular topic, we're essentially constraining the available paths for exploration, so children will continue to try exploring in the open paths. They ask plenty and plenty of questions, to the point that most adults are fried. You see, the asking of questions is just one of the means of exploration, and it's not necessarily the most natural. Asking questions doesn't seem to be an innate behavior. Physically exploring the environment seems innate. I'm imagining the kid that wants to escape the prison that is a crib. Why do we have those anyways? Oh well, I'll save that question for another piece. Getting back to the main point, I think the asking of questions as a means of exploration is something that's learned in itself from other people (probably a decent chunk from schooling where we're constantly bombarded with questions that need to be addressed).

I think this behavior of many people not thinking stems from the friction of asking a question. And there's no way around that friction. But there is a way for people to bear the burden of the friction with a greater value in sight (as per the last piece). So, the obvious question becomes: where is this friction coming from?

When we're children, there's effectively no friction to asking a question. Thinking hasn't completely developed, so we don't even understand the effort that has to go into "conscious thinking". Or perhaps our perception of the world is so naive that all thinking is actually very easy. Whatever it is (and that alone is another idea worth exploring), it's clear that it doesn't cost them much to ask questions besides the opportunity cost. What do I mean? Children seem to have a desire to run around and play. When you constrain them to a single room or even to a single seat, the environment you craft about some particular topic (or perhaps a topic they heard about recently) will make it to the top of the "interest rankings" because they can't be interested in doing other things. For example, if they're in a car, then they can't be about and running. Assuming they don't have an iPad as most kids nowadays, they might look out the window and their interest may lie within the environment outside of the window as is created by the moving car. They might ask about the other cars on the highway, or they might ask about a random tree they saw, or they might ask nothing and just indulge with their eyes (if they haven't made a habit of asking questions).

The issue comes as we grow a bit older. There's a greater expectation for us to figure out the answer to things on our own, which is ridiculously difficult. Those who cross that barrier benefit, but those who don't are left at a severe disadvantage. There's also a lot more social awareness that may dissuade students from asking questions in the same environments that they should be. There's probably a variety of other factors to explore (another piece), but the point is people stop because the friction grows. With the development of the internet and AI, there's less friction to asking questions, but there's also less pressure on developing an individual line of thought (which I think is a separate discussion to have).

I didn't get as far into this as I had liked, but I ended up landing on a couple of cool ideas along the way.
