While digging around this questions-and-thinking topic, I came across another theory: the erotetic theory of reasoning. "Erotetic" just means "of questioning." As usual, I haven't read the actual papers behind it, so everything here is the understanding I've gotten from looking into the matter through other sources.

Here's the claim: The mind's default process for reasoning treats whatever information comes in as raising a question, and it resolves that question by jumping to the most direct answer available. The one that requires the least construction. That default is fast, and it's usually fine. But it's exactly what produces the classic reasoning errors, because the most direct answer systematically ignores the alternatives. And it get's interesting when speaking about good reasoning. It isn't a different process. It's the same process with more questions forced through it. Questions that make you construct the alternatives ("what else could be true?", "what am I not considering?").

Good thinking, in this theory, is the default plus some imposed question-discipline.

I think you can find evidence of this theory all over the world, in any task that needs to be done. But rather than stretching it across everything, let's just take one good example and run with it. Chess. For context, I've played a lot of chess. I started in high school and dove pretty deep into the game (I was part of the online chess boom), so this is one of those topics where I can speak from some experience.

In chess, the deductive reasoning part is easy. Particularly around the opening stage of the game, where players typically prepare some openings based off of what the best positions to be in are. The moves are known, the responses are known, and you're mostly just executing memory. Then comes the moment where the game leaves the preparation. Players enter a new position they haven't seen before, and now they have to think about what questions to ask to evaluate the position, and then do that to an extreme level of depth to make better decisions. Watch grandmasters at a tournament and you can see the toll this takes. They spend hours calculating the moves within one particular line of thought before trying another one, and another one, and another one, to figure out what the best move is.

The erotetic theory shows up in the blunders. Every chess player knows the experience. The position suggests a move, the move looks good, you play it, and your opponent immediately shows you what you didn't consider. That's the default process without any checks (pun intended). The position raised a question ("what do I play?") and you took the most direct answer it offered.

The cool thing is that chess culture is full of tools built to fight exactly that base level of thinking. There's the classic line "when you see a good move, look for a better one". That's a rule about questions, not about moves.

Beginners get drilled on the "checks, captures, and threats" checklist. Before every move, you put those three questions to the position, whether or not anything about it bothers you.

And for deep calculation, you list the plausible moves first, then calculate each one separately. All of these do the same job. They force more questions through before settling on an answer.

Then there's the matter of what actually separates players in skill. There are some old studies I came across (from the mid-1900s) comparing masters against strong club players. The masters **didn't calculate deeper.** They didn't search more lines. The search statistics were similar across the levels. What differed was **which candidate moves occurred to them at all.** The masters' first instincts were *simply better,* so their calculation got spent on questions worth asking. In other words, what separates players in skill level is their intuition, their ability to ask the right questions.

Friendly reminder to be strategic.
