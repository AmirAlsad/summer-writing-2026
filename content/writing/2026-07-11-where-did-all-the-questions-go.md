I've been thinking a lot about questions lately. More specifically, about the difference between asking questions and thinking. My intuition tells me that thinking, at least the directed and conscious kind, can be broken down as a series of responses to implicit questions. In this piece, I want to focus on children and how they begin to think and ask questions.

It seems to me that children are apt to explore when there is some disconnect between themselves and their environment. Something doesn't match what they expected, the mismatch bothers them, and out comes a question. As I've been looking into this stuff, I found John Dewey. He's a philosopher and school reformer from about a century ago, and he called this phenomenon the "felt difficulty." The idea is that thinking doesn't start with a question. It starts with an itch. Something is off before you can even say what, and the question is the first thing the itch produces. To be clear, I haven't read his work yet (it's on my list), but this is the understanding that I've gotten from looking into the matter a bit.

There's also research out there on how much children actually ask. The numbers I came across were something like one to three questions a minute in normal conversation. That's a ridiculous amount of questions. I don't remember asking that many as a kid... The interesting thing about all these questions is that it can't be written off as just attention-seeking. There's genuine curiosity behind them. When kids get a non-answer, they re-ask, they rephrase, or they take a guess themselves and check it.

And then it stops.

The easy explanation is that the world just stops surprising you (as a child). You figure out how doors and dogs and rain work. The gaps that were once there close, and the questions fall off because the system did its job. I don't buy it though. There are so many beauties within this reality that it never really becomes the case that the world stops surprising you. I find myself asking many questions and discovering various beauties to this day. The surprise just becomes less and less obvious, requiring more and more effort.

Another reason to doubt that explanation is the simply truth that we still learn quite a lot after the age at which we stop asking all those curious little questions. During our teenage years, we learn a lot about human behavior and how people can really act. We learn how evil the world can be. And those lessons don't come from us asking questions. They just happen to us as experience.

So where does the asking go? I think a major piece of it has to be attributed to modern education, and another piece to modern societal standards. It seems to me that these things align in that they prioritize the systems functionality over the success of the individual. The consequence to that claim is that individual success requires being critical about participation within the system(s), but I won't dive into that idea now.

Let's start with school. When we go to school, we're treated like machines. You're taught something, then you're given homework on it, then you're tested on it. For only a short period of time, if at all, do you really have the opportunity to inquire more about the topic being learned. And on top of all that, school tells you that you need to learn and know these particular things, things determined by some random people far beyond the teachers (the ones who actually craft the curriculum and whatnot). Of course, as children, we're not really aware of any of this, but we don't need to be aware of something to internalize it.

Now, I don't entirely disagree with the setup. They want the system to scale, and if you want a system to scale, you have to have some structure to it. But this structure doesn't suit what's natural for us. What's natural for a child is probably to explore something because they **want to explore it.** I'm not sure a child faces any *natural* felt disturbance when learning addition or subtraction. These are abstract concepts (on their own), so it's no surprise that we have to tie them to real life (you know, the examples of people buying fruit and whatnot) before a kid can actually understand what's going on.

You see, the felt disturbance is a subjective experience, and in an ideal world, whatever each kid wants to explore is what gets dived into for that particular kid's education. I'm not saying kids should learn whatever they want to learn. Some things do matter beyond preference (like knowing how to add and subtract). But those things can also be made engaging to the child in a way that interests them. That would be most natural. School doesn't accommodate natural behavior, and that has a very high cost. A very, very high cost.

(Side tangent: this is part of why I'm a big proponent of homeschooling, under the ideal parent of course. It goes along with my general thought that most things should be localized, with the assumption that the local force is a good one. I'll admit that's not an easy case to make.)

So, what schools do is teach children that their own explorations or deviations aren't relevant or important for the most part. Instead, they have to stick to what's prescribed. Sounds a bit dystopian when I write it out, but that's how I see it.

Funny enough though, that Dewey guy shared many of these same ideas, and he ran an experimental school in Chicago around 1900, built on the idea that the material worth learning can be arranged so that the disturbance arises naturally around it. He thought the sin of conventional schooling was presenting subject matter severed from anything the child had experienced, so no difficulty is ever felt and no genuine thinking ever starts. Turns out, the experimental school worked great in getting students to learn by doing. Perhaps I'll talk more about this guy after I read some of his work.

There's also the data showing that children's question rates collapse in classrooms compared to at home. Again, not going to dive into it right now. Maybe for another time.

Then there's the social front. As kids grow older, they become, all of a sudden, a lot more concerned with what their peers think of them. Nowadays it's much more so because of social media, and all that social anxiety reflects in environments like the classroom, where children are surrounded by their peers. A question is an admission that you don't know something, and somewhere along the way, admitting that starts to feel more costly than staying quiet. I know that many of my own friends have held questions back because of the fear of being judged by others. That habit, to me, seems absolutely demoralizing. Not being able to explore, not having the freedom to do what you want to do, because you're worried about other people and what they think.

All that led me to ask: where does this idea that we should be surrounded by people our own age even come from? In a homeschool environment, you learn from your parents, and you're not going to feel socially anxious about asking your parents questions. Learning from someone a few years ahead of you in some informal setup doesn't surround you with one big cohort of same-age peers either (which is tutoring). Something about that cohort setup can really mess with the mind, I think.

It turns out the age-graded classroom is a designed standard (who would've thought!), not a natural default. From what I've gathered, American reformers in the 1840s (Horace Mann being the famous one) visited Prussia and admired its state school system, which sorted children by age into grades for administrative efficiency (at least it was efficient, unlike modern school systems which miss out on efficacy AND efficiency). Before all that though, the norm was the one-room schoolhouse. All ages mixed together, older kids helping the younger ones. To be clear, I'm not saying this is better or worse (probably worse though). I'm showcasing that the setup could be different. The grade system won because it scales. Standardized batches, standardized curriculum per batch, interchangeable teachers. A standard adopted for the system's throughput (which, again, isn't necessarily bad on it's own), but then it was naturalized until nobody remembers it was ever a choice (that's the real loss). Similar to my piece on the podcast I created surrounding the credit hour...

While on this topic, I remembered a metric I once heard from a psychology class: if a child hears lower-quality language (say, from an older sibling that's still relatively young), that child's mind is hindered (ever so slightly) because it's getting all this bad data about how to live and "survive." Something along those lines. Pressing on the idea though, it seems the research behind the idea is contested, so I'm not going to develop an argument around that evidence. If I were to develop the argument though, it would be that learning from a bunch of your peers isn't ideal in the slightest. The question then becomes about the line between socialization and learning. Anyways, I just wanted to share all that for thought transparency.

So, to sum up, the school story and the peer story do different kinds of damage. School goes after the components that turns the itch into a question by teaching the child that the itch itself isn't relevant. The social pressure goes after the voicing, where the question still forms but gets swallowed. You could argue the second is less damaging, since a swallowed question isn't a dead one. But thoughts are short-term. If you make a habit of suppressing them, then you may never have the opportunity, or may never learn, to really explore those ideas, even when in private. Especially nowadays, with all the distractions that exist within society waiting to fill the recently freed mental space.
