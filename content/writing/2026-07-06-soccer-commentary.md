I recently watched a video on Peter Drury's commentary of the World Cup final in 2022. I normally don't watch such content, but I've been thinking about a potential project (that I'll explain more on in just a bit) and I figured I could write off the video as "analysis".

A quick tangent though: in retrospect, that wording that came to my mind, "write off the ... as ...", is typically used in the context of taxes, where we, the people, fulfill an obligation to the government by giving up some of our resources. The whole idea of writing something off in regard to taxes revolves around using legal methods to adapt the obligation that any one of us, the people, holds in giving up some of our resources. The most common example is taking an expense that is in our interest, and if it aligns with the interest of the government, then we effectively "write" that expense as serving the obligation. In my case, my leisurely desires (or "self") "wrote off" an interesting video as being in the interest of my work/growth/purpose desires. Anyways, let's get back to it.

Now, I want to talk about soccer (or futbol, but I'll be saying soccer as an American) commentary, and I want to talk about soccer in particular because it's what I'm most familiar with (i.e. I don't know if the ideas expressed here will extend to other commentaries). With soccer commentary, I keep thinking about the emotion and the dramatic storytelling. Some commentators are absolutely terrible at it, and others are absolutely remarkable. Some have the ability to engage the audience, tending to do so regardless of the theatrics of the game, which is what separates a decent commentator from a great one.

The first thing that came to my mind was that there's no way the arts of soccer commentary can be taken care of by a machine. The clearest example I can point to is the FC games (which used to simply be called FIFA before licensing issues) from EA Sports. They have commentary, but it's nowhere near as dramatic and engaging as what you see in the real world. At least I don't think so. I haven't been exposed to FC commentary that was dramatic and that actually pulled me in.

Upon further review though, pun intended, I wouldn't hold onto the claim that machines can't do it, especially as we're seeing machines take care of lots of things nowadays. You see, a lot of what a commentator does is exactly the sort of thing I'd expect a machine to handle. Knowing the players. Knowing the history between two clubs. Having the stats ready. Even finding the right words for a big moment, the pure wordcraft of it. None of that strikes me as "exceptionally human."

Perhaps the "exceptionally human" piece is how they do all of it live. Calling the match as it happens, with no idea what's coming next. The commentator is finding out what happens at the same moment you are, and reacting to it in real-time. To be clear, it's not that machines can't act in real-time. They very much can, and in many respects they are more "real-time" than humans. But performing "real-time" as a human seems to hold some deeper value....

These commentators are obviously biased in their own ways, and in some ways it's funny. Sometimes they'll make a bad call, they'll give their opinions, and part of the whole experience is that you're excited as a reflection of their excitement. At least when you're watching at home. In the actual stadium, it's the excitement of everyone around you that makes you excited. It's an interesting idea. Personally, I've never been to a soccer game, so I've never felt that shared excitement. I can only speak to the version that comes through a screen.

If you know it's AI, regardless of whether the quality is the same as that of a human, then you know that the excitement is artificial, and that ruins the experience (at least I think it would). I'm seeing it as analogous to those who live life **truly believing** that there is absolutely NO free will. The reality is the same, but we just perceive it differently.
