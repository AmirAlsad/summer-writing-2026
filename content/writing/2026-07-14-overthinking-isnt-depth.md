There's a crowd of people that does little thinking. They don't ask many questions before coming to a decision, and they tend to make pretty poor, pretty rash decisions. They don't pay much thought to matters. Then there's the crowd that overthinks. The overthinking crowd's problem isn't that they think too much. Don't get me wrong, they do. But it's rarely ever a matter of depth, and probably very frequently a matter of cyclical reasoning, in which they'll ask the same questions over and over, with their emotions influencing their perception.

These two crowds look like opposites, but I'd say they're the same failure at different settings. Both are bad at managing their questions.

The rash decider is the erotetic default running unchecked. A situation raises a question, the most direct answer arrives, and they take it. Zero additional questions confronted. Off they go.

The overthinker is the trickier case. They keep making interrogative moves addressed to an oracle that cannot answer. "What if I had said something different?" "What will they think of me?" "Was that the right call?" No experiment, no observation, no memory can return a verdict on those. So the game never advances. The same question gets asked again, and again, with the emotions doing their part in keeping it alive. Plenty of moves get made, but no new information ever arrives.

The interesting case is the decision. The overthinker stuck on some decision ("should I take the job?", "should I launch the thing?") keeps interrogating their own imagination, running the scenarios over and over. But for a question like "will this work?", imagination is the wrong oracle. The only oracle that can actually answer is reality, and the only way to put the question to reality is to make the move. That reframes what acting even is. The decision to act isn't the end of the inquiry. It's an interrogative move itself, and it's the only move left once the internal oracles have been exhausted. Reality tells you all that you need to know, you just gotta be listening.

Chess players get help with this that the rest of life doesn't offer: the clock. A grandmaster can't calculate forever. At some point, the clock forces them to play the best move they've found rather than the best move that exists. Everyday life doesn't display a clock, and maybe that's part of why the overthinker never stops. Nothing external ever forces the move.

I think writing helps in this way. By writing something down, you make it painfully obvious when you're just asking the same question over and over again. In your head, each pass through the loop can feel like progress. On paper, the second and third appearances of the same question are sitting right there, undeniable. That's why writing things down, getting stuff on paper, is a really helpful method to inspire action.
