I've struggled to appreciate art. Music less so, but it still falls within the general realm of the arts. Literature, more so. I've been able to gather more appreciation for it. And when I look at where my appreciation actually goes, it goes to complex systems built by engineers rather than to that which comes from art. I've been thinking about that for a while. I don't really care for art or music in my day-to-day, so this isn't me trying to fix anything. I'm just trying to understand why my appreciation lies where it does.

I don't think it's because I have a natural preference for engineering. I think I just understand the question that it's addressing better. That framing comes from a philosopher named R.G. Collingwood. His claim was that a statement is only intelligible as an answer to some question, and that you can't understand a thing (a text, an artifact, a claim) without recovering the question it was built to answer. Knowledge, for him, doesn't come in propositions. It comes in question-answer pairs, and a proposition floating free of its question is only half an object.

With engineering, the problem is pretty damn clear, and the complexity is in the execution of the solution (typically). The question ships with the deliverable. It's in the spec, the problem statement, the README. And this holds even for the productive accidents. Plenty of times in engineering, you're tackling one problem and end up solving a completely different one through some tangential discovery. But notice that even then, the question arrives statable. The classic story is penicillin: nobody was looking for it, but the moment the mold cleared the bacteria, the question it answered ("what kills bacteria?") could be stated instantly, publicly, by anyone.

In literature, I think it's easier than in the rest of the arts. There's much more behind it. Language carries the story, so the question is at least recoverable from the text itself. With music, there are sometimes lyrics, and you can kinda tell a story behind it. A lot of times, you can't. Maybe it's just me. And in the case of paintings, it's very difficult for me to discern what question the thing is answering unless there's something that gives it away in the piece or some explanation out there. If it's not clear, I'm not gonna be able to tell what the artist was trying to do. I'm just taking a guess, and the guess isn't really a meaningful question.

The extreme case is abstract art, where I'm not even sure I can call it art, because it's just a bunch of shapes put together and the meaning is whatever each individual makes of it. Frankly, I'm out of my league here, but part of me wants to say that whole business is just nonsense, particularly around the idea of critiquing something that's subjective. Those who do it as a job have a reason to spout nonsense. The interpretations have to keep coming, whether or not there's anything there to recover.

That said, I do think one can develop their analysis of any particular form. I have a friend who is really tuned in to classical music. He's spent a lot of time in that space. When he tells me about a piece he's listened to or a composer he's into, I trust that he understands what he's talking about and that he's not just spouting nonsense. He has no reason to. Nobody's paying him for interpretations.

I think when a lot of people engage with art, they enjoy it because of the "structural" benefits. With music, people like the beats and the patterns. Tension and release. Expectation and violation. People don't really pay attention to the lyrics. Think of all the times that you thought a song said one thing but it really says a completely different thing, or think of all the times when you just mumbled along to a catchy song without really caring for the lyrics. With visual art, it's rarer to find people that enjoy looking at art. People that enjoy creating art is a different case, and in that, I would say the "structural" benefit is the expression of oneself.

The point here is that there doesn't have to be a question recovered or some "meaning" involved for people to enjoy it. In that, I would separate enjoyment from appreciation. I can like music with a beat because there's something innate in me that responds to the beat. I can tap my foot along to it. It's catchy, addicting, dopamine-inducing. But that's not the same as appreciation of meaning. If it was the case that we conflated the two, you could say I "appreciate" my water bottle because it carries my water around. But I'm just using the water bottle. I'm "enjoying" the functional utility, but there is no deeper meaning to me.

The cleanest case I have of both channels firing at once is [Gnomo Palomo](/gnomo-palomo). I'm entertained (the "enjoyment" from the absurdity), and at the same time, the questions behind the content are fully legible to me. I can tell exactly what it's doing, for whom, and how much craft went into it, because I'm inside the reference pool it draws from. Looking back at that piece, my appreciation of Gnomo was the "appreciation" doing the heavy lifting.

Getting back to the original separation of engineering and the different forms of art, I think there's two perspectives as to why my appreciation differs among the forms. The structural perspective says it's a fact about the media themselves. Engineering ships its question, literature carries it in language, music and painting make you reconstruct it from very little. The experience perspective says the questions behind paintings are just as recoverable as any others, and my valuation is simply a reflection for where my attention has already been spent (i.e. I haven't spent time engaging with music/art, I've spent some time reading literature, and I've spent the most time engaging in engineering). I think both perspectives are true at the same time.
