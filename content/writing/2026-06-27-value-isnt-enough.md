I've been thinking about this concept of "genuine value."

Listening to Alex Hormozi, he tells entrepreneurs that it's all about perceived value. That's the fundamental output of the value equation in *$100M Offers*. And he's right (as far as I'm aware) for what he's describing. If your intention is to grow and scale a business, then perceived value is all that you need.

But perceived value runs into some problems the moment you leave the world of business. If your intention is to do good and improve the world, you start to run into some difficulties.

There's definitely a lot of overlap between doing good and providing value. No doubt about that. But there's many times that the perceived value of something isn't actually good for the people valuing it.

Take hard drugs for instance (since people love to talk about the benefits of the softer ones). Those who are addicted will have a high perceived value when they're trying to get their next fix. So, under the framing of purely providing value, we ought to build a drug empire. And to be fair to Hormozi, he isn't justifying all business with perceived value. He's just describing how to build a successful business, irrespective of what that business does.

Now, plenty of those same addicts would pay good money to *not* want the thing, so the perceived value is actually on both sides, depending on the persons state. The same person holds a high perceived value on the fix and a high perceived value on being free of it. Two valuations, same person, pointing opposite ways.

The cleanest way to see it is the business you could build on top of it. One that sells the drug AND the rehab. It maximizes perceived value on both ends, and it's still morally wrong in my view. So perceived value, even when you've got it on both sides, doesn't get you to good.

Some savvy businessmen might draw the line at "legal." But that's not a justified line, in my opinion. By sticking to what's legal, you're just claiming the government as a higher moral entity. You can claim what you want, but you're still morally responsible for that which you do.

Only when God, Glorified and Exalted is He, forbids something does it become impermissible. Not when there's a law against that particular thing. Now, I'm not saying one should break the laws.

I would never say that...

The laws do hold weight, but only as an agreement between individuals and their governments, and upholding one's agreements is itself part of the moral bounds defined by God, Glorified and Exalted is He. The law isn't absolute though. If it ever transgresses that absolute moral boundary, then it shouldn't be followed. And anything that goes beyond the law is subject to those same absolute boundaries regardless.

Digressing from all that, my point is simple. Just because something is legal AND/OR that people value it doesn't mean it's **good**.

It's with that reasoning that I can claim the gambling industry (whether it's casinos, sports betting, or prediction markets) is bad for society. I'm not going to argue that one fully here, I'm just making the claim.

So if perceived value isn't enough, and legal isn't the line, what actually is genuine value?

I'd say genuine value is perceived value that is at least not morally bad.

The reason the bar sits at "not bad" and not "actually good" comes down to what a business is. As a business, you can't enforce what's good on other people. That's something that governments do (which to be clear, I'm not saying they should be), because in the case of governments enforcing what's good, the consumer's input (i.e. the citizen) doesn't matter in the larger scope of things. Simply, maintaining a good society isn't incumbent on the market. The market runs on the consumer explicitly agreeing to what they're engaging in.

I'm not asking a business to make anyone better. It's not its job, and it couldn't pull it off even if it wanted to (because people tend to be uninformed about what's good for them). What I'm asking is that it doesn't harm the people who engage with it. The business has a responsibility to not harm those that engage with it, and the consumer agrees into everything past that.

The obvious bad cases (the drugs, the gambling) are easy to point at. The real tension exists in the stuff that sits in this conventionally thought of as "harmless" but is everywhere in our everyday lives. Games, social media, the entertainment industry as a whole (it may seem slightly off that I'm lumping social media with the entertainment industry, but I'll explain this more in a later piece if I don't change my mind by then). Smoking used to be in this category (not entertainment, but the "harmless" one) until people realized the negative health effects. These things looks harmless, but I suspect there's more harm in it than people give it credit for.
