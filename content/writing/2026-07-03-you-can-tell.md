Anthropic is an AI company. It spends a lot making sure it doesn't feel like one. The color is a warm terracotta (credit to Minecraft for teaching me what that is). The illustrations look hand-drawn, a little unpolished on purpose. And the product has a human first name, "Claude", compated to a lineup of ChatGPT and Gemini and Grok. None of that is an accident. It was built, deliberately, as a counter to the cold, clinical default of every other AI product. It's not just them though. There seems to have been a whole trend of earthy, made-by-a-human aesthetics, showing up specifically as a reaction to the flood of AI.

A sentiment starts in people's guts. It surfaces first in art and fashion (the fast-moving, low-commitment spaces), then hardens into the products we buy every day when the art and fashion show solid results. Is this a real, lasting shift in what people want? Or is it a fad that burns off as soon as AI stops feeling new?

Most new technologies don't get backlash. People DO have to adjust tho. Steel is a good example. When it came in, it displaced iron and wood and put some craftsmen out of work, but nobody wrote manifestos about the soul of the blacksmith. It was a material swap. It never touched anyone's sense of self. AI is different in that sense. It does touch that sense of self. It carries with it own's own fear of being made redundant.

To be clear, feelings are a bad instrument here. People say they hate AI content and then consume it all day without ever checking who made it. So, we need to be a bit more critical. When someone consumes a piece of content, what do they actually care about? We can conduct a quick little thought experiment. Take the content and swap out who made it. Either a different person or a machine. Then, ask whether the content still holds the initial value.

Sometimes it does. Nobody asks whether their tax software was written with love. If it files your taxes, it files your taxes, and who wrote it is nothing but noise. The value lives entirely in the output. We can call that **instrumental.**

Sometimes it doesn't. Swap the maker and the whole thing breaks down, even though nothing on the surface changed. You're not going to take serious career advice from a little kid. The maker is a direct part of the good. We can call that **constitutive.**

One core case where provenance truly matters is when you can't verify the good yourself. Think of the difference between a joke versus a documentary. A joke's proof is whether or not you laughed. Sometimes the person that told the joke matters, but that's normally just integrated into the telling of the joke. So, assuming ideal conditions, if it's a good joke, you'll laugh regardless of where it came from. A documentary, on the other hand, makes claims about the world that you can't directly verify. You're kinda just trusting that the maker of the documentary isn't lying their arse off. So the identity of the maker matters in this case because there's concern of trust.

I've seen some interesting content created with AI. A cute cat or a baby, plus some absurd twist. The baby doing a backflip off a rooftop, and the cat in a suit closing a business deal. Cute-plus-absurd. It's something that AI is fantastic at. The absurd part is hard for a human to pull off and trivial for a generator to conjure. And nobody gives a crap who's making it. 100% instrumental. The machine wins here, and it should, because it's all about the destination, not the journey.

Gnomo Palomo is similar. An absurdist AI gnome focused purely on comedy. But, it's different in a critical way. It's not raw absurdity. No one enjoys that deranged nonsense. It's *pointed* absurdity, meaning it has some direction, and that direction comes from the person's taste and from the culture.

Now, let's consider human skits. To me, they seem the farthest from being impacted by AI content-wise. These are real people being awkward together. There are mess-ups, bloopers, odd timing, etc. These aren't things that we're seeing AI bring out, and I would probably bet that it doesn't end up simulating human behavior in that regard, even though that goes against my typical bets of AI development. So, skits are constitutive.

Then, there's Fern. High-production animated documentaries that are human-crafted (at least as of the time of this writing), and there's genuine value in that level of detail. The craft itself isn't what'll keep Fern distinct in the long-run. We can assume that AI produces high-quality animations in the near future. What can't be dismissed is that they, Fern, actually did the reporting. The idea that the research is real, and that they aren't making it up. That's why Fern videos have to come from Fern and not some indie YouTubers. The journalism is what makes it constitutive, and it probably works the same way we have respect for the New York Times or the Washington Post.

Currently, there are goods that lie in the middle between instrumental and constitutive. These goods are *intrinsically* instrumental, but there can be some sentiment bolted onto a point of provenance that takes advantage of people's current sentiments. Think of a blog header image. Nobody needs to know who drew it. It's instrumental. But there are plenty of people that "won't pay for AI" in a way that makes "made by a human" become a feature that can be charged for. I don't think these goods will last. Economics will make it a brainless decision to use AI, and people will get used to the new technology. It's that simple.

So the human gets "demoted" from the default to the boutique. The hand-thrown mug in a world of machined ones. Still there and still wanted by the people who care enough to seek it out. But no longer the expected output.
