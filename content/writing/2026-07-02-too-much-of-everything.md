Plenty of people can be a creator nowadays. The barrier to entry has dropped dramatically. I'm exploring the idea of becoming one myself by writing a piece every day of the summer and pushing it all out (though I'm still procrastinating the part where I put it onto social media). I'm part of the stampede of people that are signing up to produce content, and this piece is my thinking about how the stampede will be received.

There's a broader sentiment that's behind my intuition within this domain: there is too much of everything. Too many posts, too many videos, too many newsletters, too many people with a personal brand. Perhaps it's just me being overwhelmed by the true size of the market, and it likely is just my feeling at the end of the day. In other words, it's not really too much because there is healthy competition and a clear demand for all this content. Nevertheless, I'll explore the concept of content exploding in supply.

I've been thinking about the idea that AI can't counterfeit a "someone." It can fake the artifact; the video, the essay, maybe even the voice (but in my experience that never quite works out); but it can't be a real person with all the (beautiful) risks of staying alive. And so there is some value, generally speaking, when some content is "made by a person" versus "made by an AI." And so, the category of human-produced content as a whole is expected to grow (the "creator economy" concept, which I first heard from Alex Hormozi). That doesn't mean that individuals that create human-produced content are going to be fine and dandy. There are plenty of regular people that are entering the content creation space, as suggested by "the stampeded" earlier, and so within human-produced content the competition will only grow.

It's a bit funny. I've never been more valuable as "a human writer" and never more replaceable as *this* human writer.

There's something to be said about programmers nowadays. Imagine the kid who learns to code right as AI gets good at coding. They're not going to be needed (unless they're remarkable), because AI can produce much better software at a faster rate (and soon, a cheaper rate too), and there's (in theory) a limited amount of demand for software.

It's not the same case for content creation, primarily because there's the premise that AI can't be "a someone" as mentioned earlier. However, efficiency through AI combined with the massive amount of people producing content can certainly lead to a disgusting amount of content being put out there.

Let's switch gears a bit and talk about Thomas Malthus. An economist that came to a depressing conclusion in the 1700s. Population was growing exponentially, doubling and doubling again. Food was growing arithmetically, one field added at a time. Eventually, the fast one overtakes the slow one, and when it does, there are more mouths than there is food. He suggests that famine, disease, and war are what will have to keep the population in check to what the land can sustain.

Three things have to be true for "Malthusian thought." A ceiling that barely moves, something growing fast against it, and a collision that gets corrected through suffering.

Let's map it onto the content situation we were describing earlier. The food is attention. The total amount of human attention in the world, which is close to fixed (only so many people with only so many hours each). The population is us. The someones producing content. Every new creator, every new brand, every person deciding that they will finally start posting. The food barely grows. The population explodes. Take a gander at what the outcome will be.

Even I have to admit when I'm being a bit dramatic. The outcome is nothing like famine, disease, or war. It's just a change in the competitive landscape. But this change probably won't impact everyone "in a balanced manner." It'll route more attention to the tiny number of people that already take almost all of it, and the new kids will have to deal with attention scraps. That's my prediction at least. It's incredibly important to note that Malthus was wrong. The mouths never outran the food. We got fertilizer, tractors, irrigation, crops bred to yield more per acre (search up the Green Revolution). The famines he predicted didn't arrive on schedule. He assumed no technological progress.

But what if we're mistaken? What if human attention actually can expand and I'm just thinking in a primitive manner? Perhaps in the same way that there's advanced technology used to farm the land, we could create advanced technology to farm our attention. Maybe the work Elon is doing with Neuralink can help us get there!

I'm obviously being satirical, but as with most satire, there's a depressing reality behind it.

Say we do it anyway. Say Neuralink, or whatever comes after it, actually works, and we learn to farm attention, to make it abundant. It's a self-defeating endeavor. The reason a person's attention is worth anything is that it's scarce. You only have so much of it, so spending it on someone *means* something. Make it abundant and you devalue it. Kinda like printing money.
