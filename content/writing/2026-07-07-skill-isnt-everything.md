As I was thinking about soccer commentary (yesterday), I figured that the skill it takes to perform it at a high level puts it beyond the scope of a machine taking over (in practice at least, theory is a whole other case). Then I noticed I have the same instinct about comedy. I haven't seen something genuinely funny come out of an AI. The greatest contribution that AI has had to comedy is being the laughing stock that comedians make fun of. Getting back to the point, I'm not sure exactly what makes it so difficult for an AI to do. Maybe it's that we're conditioned to laugh at content produced by other humans AND to laugh with other humans (only in rare cases have I laughed by myself, and those cases typically involve me thinking of an earlier funny moment).

Perhaps it's that comedy revolves around the defiance of expectations. When a kid says something out of pocket, or when a pet does something silly, we laugh. And AI isn't quite known for acting out of pocket. And if it does act out of pocket (which it never seems able to do naturally), it's because it was instructed to do so, which definitely takes away from some of the effect. Part of the consideration goes back to the perception that it's coming from a machine, as I mentioned towards the end of my last piece.

Then my mind went to other forms of art. Music, poetry, and so on, have been "attacked", in some way or another, by the development of AI. Anyone can create music nowadays, but the music industry seems just fine to me. So, if the machine can produce the thing in question, why does some of it still feel like it holds value and some of it doesn't?

The first thing I want to get out of the way is that AI, as far as I can tell, can only really impact the skills market. And the skills market isn't a direct reflection of value. We tend to blur those two together, at least I do, but they aren't the same thing. In other words, the difficulty it takes to serve something doesn't wholly reflect the value that something holds.

Let's bring back the music example. For most of history, making a decent song took a skill that not many people had, and part of why a song was worth anything was exactly that. Not many people could make one. The scarcity of the skill was a core part of the value of the thing it produced. Nowadays, anyone can create music. Not just music, but a whole song with merely a prompt. You type out what you want and a full track comes back. The scarcity is gone, and so is the value that came from that scarcity. A generic AI generation is worth almost nothing. But not all music lost its value when the skill became widespread. A song a person writes for someone they love didn't lose anything at all. So, there must be some other component that contributed to the value. As I've thought about it for a bit, value seems to come from who's doing the action, the scarcity of the skills being used, and to whom it's being done for, all together. Skill is only one of the three.


## Who and For Whom

Think about Elon. His skills are rare on their own, but that's not what makes him stand out (at least I don't think so). He's an autistic visionary building for humanity as a whole. Imagine if he wasn't autistic, or wasn't a visionary. Imagine if he was just building for other autistic people. Who he is and what he's trying to do go hand in hand with the value people ascribe to him.

Same thing with a lot of art (as we covered with the music example earlier). My nephew recently drew some interestingly shaped hearts, colored them, and gave one to each of his uncles and aunties. It wasn't Picasso-level, whatever that means, but I still ascribe a lot of value to it. Because it was *from him* and I know he put effort into it *for me.* To be clear, that isn't the same as a work of art with a meaning or purpose that I, or anyone else, could resonate with. Those are two different things.

To really drive the point home, a decent chunk of Alex Hormozi's content is just him talking to business owners and telling them how they can improve their business. Many of them pay to attend his Acquisition.com workshops even though there's plenty of free content online. I could say the same things he says, with the same business-dissection skills, and it would be worth less. Because he, Alex Hormozi, is the one saying it to them directly. The value there isn't about the skill at all, and it isn't even about the effort (because it seems easy for him to do). It's about the **who.**

If I'm onto something with all this, then a lot of art (at least the art that doesn't carry a meaning or purpose we can resonate with) gets its value misattributed. We credit the object, but the value was really coming from the skill it took to make it. Though I'll admit, as far as I'm aware, all art is created with some meaning or purpose, even if that meaning is the absence of meaning in life as a whole. Why does that matter? I'm not entirely sure... I just know it's the case.

We can swap the maker of these things for someone, or something, else that produces the exact same output, and see if the value survives. In the case of my nephew's hand-drawn heart, an identical heart from a stranger means nothing to me. So, the value there was clearly never the object. It was him. Now, when it comes to some background music, the value detaches from the maker. I just don't care who made the background music.


## Lining Up with SPCL

At some point, I realized my thoughts lined up oddly well with a framework I heard about recently. Hormozi has this thing he calls SPCL, which is about building influence through content. It stands for status, power, credibility, and likeness.

Status is straightforward enough.

Power is the vaguest of the four, and Hormozi defines it by say-do correspondence. When someone does what you say and something positive comes of it, they're more likely to comply with the next thing you say. So power is effectively the ability for what you say or do to bring about something positive. And that ties directly to skill, or skill and knowledge at least. Power is basically demonstrated skill.

Credibility is the record of having pulled it off before. It's what lets someone trust you without checking your work first. Tends to be tied to status, but there are many cases in which it isn't.

Likeness is relatability. The sense that this person is like you, that you'd get along, that you get where they're coming from.

Both frames emphasize the state of the person providing and the relationship to the person receiving, with the ability to perform being just one component. Status and credibility focus on the "who" piece, while likeness focuses on the "to whom" piece. What makes this connection especially interesting is that these frames were seemingly built for different reasons. SPCL is a recipe for building influence from scratch. The framing I was creating came from asking what value survives when a machine can do the work. Though, the connection makes perfect sense if we say that building influence is just a function of providing value.


## What AI Actually Touches

Let's take Hormozi's framework and see how AI impacts each of the four components.

Starting with credibility, it doesn't really apply with AI. You see, credibility is just skill with proof attached. You need an accomplishment to point to, and if AI can do the thing you're referencing, it isn't even an accomplishment anymore (to some extent). On a different level, we take it for granted that AI is credible. That it knows what it's talking about and that it has the skills. Simply, we're not going to consider AI impact on credibility

Moving on to power/skill, it's clearly what gets hit the hardest. Not that it's completely out of the picture forever, but it's the one that AI impacts the most.

Status is positional. A seat, a scarcity of position. AI doesn't hand out seats, unless you own an AI company (joke), so this one is basically untouched.

Likeness is a legitimately interesting case. If I wanted to be boring and rash, then I would just dismiss any impact. But I'm not going to do that.

One portion of "likeness" is a real, two-way relationship. An actual bond between two people who both know the other exists. My nephew is the clean case. Nothing else can be your nephew. The specific person is part of what the thing even is, so there's nothing to swap in. AI doesn't touch this at all.

The other portion is the one-directional variant. A parasocial sort of relationship. It's the closeness a follower feels toward someone who has no idea they exist. For example, celebrities and their fanbases. Notice that these cases were never **real** relationships. The follower feels the bond, but there's nobody on the other end holding up their side of it (sort of, I'm sure that celebrities feel some connection to their general audience as opposed to each particular fan). So, there's a manufactured feeling of closeness.

That's exactly why AI can step into it. If the thing was already a one-way feeling of closeness that somebody manufactured, then a machine producing that same feeling isn't doing something new. It's just automating a supply that people were already producing by hand. And we're already seeing it. People are forming real attachments to AI personas and companion apps, feeling a closeness toward something that, unlike an influencer, isn't even a person on the other end. I'm not going to go further on this topic because there's a lot that needs to be considerd. Perhaps in a later piece.

Anyways, the key takeaway here is that AI impacts skill the hardest.


## Skill is the Engine

When thinking about SPCL, power (or skill) isn't just an independent component. It's the engine that generates the other three over time. You do effective things, and from that people come to trust you (credibility), status builds up, and eventually you develop an identity that people can relate to (likeness). Skill is how a person with nothing bootstraps the rest. So when AI commoditizes skill, it's also commoditizing the build-up to the other three.

It's tempting to say that some future, more-capable variant of AI will close the door on "making it out." The whole permanent underclass concept. But that's a bit dramatic, and it isn't quite right. AI can't do *everything.* It makes "the climb" more difficult in some respect and easier in others. What actually matters is being able to take the boost it gives you and make something of it.

It's also justlogically unreasonable to say AI destroys human value. Value is subjective. When it's lost in one respect, the emphasis just moves to the factors that remain. The value doesn't vanish. It relocates onto the who and the for whom (what many refer to "distribution").
