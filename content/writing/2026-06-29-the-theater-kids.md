I wrote about social media in my last piece. One of the core ideas was that the people producing content are sort of like jesters for the masses. It's a bit dramatic, but a good analogy is a good analogy 🤷‍♂️. No hate to all the jesters out there though (I may soon become one myself).

Thinking about the jesters, I was wondering who's actually built to be one. The answer that came to me, weirdly, was the theater kids. The ones who took theater and all the adjacent classes in high school, maybe even majored in it in college/uni. They seem oddly well-suited for this whole thing. It's a bit funny because being a theater kid was a running joke "back in my day" 👴. It still might be. But, for a long time, it was something people got laughed at for.

It was before my time, but popular media and stories from other people have told me a similar tale around the computer science kids. There was a time they got made fun of for being nerds. They were glued to their computers, not going out, fascinated by something that nobody else cared about. Then the world changed with some massive technological developments and they became cool. And because it became cool, everyone started to do it. And now that everyone's in it, there's all this pessimism floating around it. The whole AI coding agents discussions that are happening nowadays.

The tale is that the ones who got mocked end up holding something valuable later on. Just as it was with the CS kids, I'll venture to say it's happening with the theater kids as well.

I suppose we could ask whether it's the theater that made them apt to be jesters or whether theater just collected the people who already had the relevant traits. Probably a bit of both. I don't really know, and I don't really care too much. Let's get back to the main point.

I should probably clarify why I think theater kids are the answer. The first reason is that they've already paid "the cringe tax." Getting up in front of people and risking looking like an idiot. They did that over and over, on purpose, back when it was uncool. Most people never pay this tax and it becomes a barrier later on (I think I'm one of these people). That middle-school instinct to "not be cringe" never leaves them, and it quietly keeps them off the stage forever. The second reason is the actual fundamentals of performing. Projection, timing, knowing how a thing reads from the outside. Real, learnable skills. One of those core skills being able to understand an identity and present it. You see, everyone develops an identity. That part isn't special. In fact, it's almost impossible not to have an identity as long as you're **a real person.** What's *not* universal is being able to see your own identity clearly and put it out there. That's a skill, and a powerful one, because even if your life is completely boring and normal, if you understand the identity and can perform it, that identity becomes useful.

When I go back to AI, as my thoughts tend to nowadays, I notice people keep reaching for "human-made" as a moat. I don't think it is. I already wrote about Gnomo Palomo, which is entirely AI-generated and still pulls real attention. I think the actual moat is that there's **someone** there. A real self that's taking the risk of putting themselves out there. An identity that you can attach to. That got me to the idea that content creation isn't all about performance. There's another component to it, and I'll call it showcasing. It's showcasing your true identity, not an artificial one (because that would make it performance), in a manner that allows others to connect with you as a creator. A performance optimizes for **attention**. A showcase optimizes for **loyalty**.

Gnomo Palomo is all about performance. A creator like Alex Hormozi is all about the showcase. He talks about the times that he's struggled with his business and what exactly led him to success. Gnomo Palomo entertains, but Hormozi changes businesses (and subsequently the lives of those that run the businesses). You see, attention can't change lives. Loyalty can, because loyalty runs on believing they've lived your experience and can take you somewhere with it.

To be clear, I'm not throwing performance under the bus as a "lesser" thing. It's not. I love Gnomo Palomo so much I wrote a whole piece on it. I think, for individuals creating a personal brand, it's usually the on-ramp for gathering attention before any of the deeper stuff can happen. Plenty of people grab attention by being entertaining and then convert it into something more.

So, where does that leave the theater kids? In theory, they're well-positioned for entering the content creation scene. They've paid the cringe tax, they've got the fundamentals, they can present an identity even out of a boring life.

It's important to understand though. You can perform your way into the room. I'm not sure you can perform your way into being trusted. Probably not to the point of changing lives (for the better).
