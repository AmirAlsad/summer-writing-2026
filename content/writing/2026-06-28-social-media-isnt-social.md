I open the app to check on my friends posts. A few minutes later I'm watching some guy I've never met do something stupid, and I've forgotten why I came in.

You get onto it to socialize with others, and you stay because of all the other content. Content that has nothing to do with your social circle. It's so prevalent that we come up with special terms for the behavior.


## Starting with the Name

I like to dive deep into the literal meaning behind certain things. Oftentimes, it'll produce some benefit to understanding the object at hand.

**Social media.** There's a "social" part, which points at socialization, and a "media" part, which points at a medium. Two different things bolted into one word. And there are two different kinds of value hiding behind them. Some of what's on there is valuable because of *who* it's tied to. Your cousin's baby photo isn't a good photo. It's good because it's your cousin's. The person is the point. Then there's the other kind: valuable no matter who made it. A funny clip is funny coming from a total stranger. The content is the point. The first kind is the "social." The second is the "media."

When the web first went "social," it didn't only mean you could talk to your friends through a screen. It meant the crowd became the ones making everything. Content stopped coming down to you from a few studios and networks and started coming up from regular people. We were the audience and the producers now, both at once.


## The Weld, and How it Came Apart

The thing everyone pictures when they say "social media" (early Facebook) was the exact moment those two values were welded together. You were broadcasting, sure, but you were broadcasting to a graph of people you actually knew. The content and the people were the same thing. Your feed *was* your friends. Then the weld came apart. Slowly, the graph stopped mattering. The feed quietly stopped serving you the people you knew and started serving you strangers picked to hold your attention. The "social" half withered and the "media" half ate everything. All because the "social" half wasn't enough.

There's this person I came across in a YouTube video, talking about how they spent five years off social media and ditched their smartphone for a flip phone. At one point they mention rewatching *The Social Network* and end up saying "Zuckerberg is a scammer by nature." That's clear enough in the movie, but ironically, you can also just watch it in what he builds. Facebook went from a relational thing to a broadcasting thing. No one asked for the switch, but it happened anyways. It was arguably inevitable (since we live in a capitalist society with an incompetent government) and it permanently changed how we all interact with social media.


## What we Say vs. What we Do

Okay, but maybe I'm just being cynical. Maybe people really are there for each other on social media. So, let's look at the numbers. Ask people why they use social media and the top answer is still "to keep in touch with friends and family." So far the promise holds. But it's only about half of users who say it, and the average person actually lists more than four separate reasons for being there. "Filling spare time" sits right behind it as the second most common reason, and for the younger crowd (16 to 24) it's named by 42.7% of them. Reading the news is up there too, around a third.

Despite "keeping in touch" being the thing people *say*, fewer than half of them even follow the friends, family, and people they actually know in real life. So what *do* they follow? Entertainers. Actors, comedians, and performers rank second among followed accounts; meme and entertainment pages rank third. The thing people say they're there for and the thing they actually do point in different directions. They show up for connection with others (the classic "what's your instagram?" line) and stay for strangers who entertain them.

I think it's obviously true, but I had to reinforce the claim. Social media is nowadays primarily used as entertainment by its users. Look at the people making the content. All they do is entertain. Sure, there's a minority putting out educational stuff, but we have to be honest here and think about how much people are really learning. You could say they're learning about the predominant digital culture. That's true, but not much beyond that.


## Follow the Money

The system was designed for it from the start. There's no way anyone could monetize a purely local sort of connection. You and your friend texting back and forth is worth nothing to anyone but you and your friend. There's no money in the "social" (except if you harness data out of it... like WhatsApp). The money is all in the "media."

These platforms are running a market with two sides and a product in the middle. The product is you. There are roughly three groups: the people who consume the content, the people who produce it, and the people who run the ads. The advertisers are the paying customers. You, the consumer, are not the customer. You're the inventory. Your attention is the thing getting sold. And the producers are the bait. They manufacture the stuff that holds you on the hook long enough for that attention to be worth something. It's a thin slice of people doing the producing. The rough rule online is that about 1% of people create most of the content, around 9% chip in once in a while, and the other 90% just watch. So, the entire feed that swallows everyone's evenings is being generated by a sliver of us, then refined and resold to the rest.

Once you see the machine this way, the weld coming apart stops looking like a betrayal and starts looking like simple math. The platform's whole job is to harvest as much of your attention as it can and auction it off. So every slot in your feed is a tiny contest for your eyeballs, and your friend's lunch photo is in that contest against the single most engaging creator on the planet, tuned specifically to you. Your friend loses. Every time. Nobody sat in a room and decided to evict your friends out of malice (I hope not at least). The machine just kept showing you whatever held you longest, ran that test a few billion times a day, and your friends just lost. The relationships were collateral.

The story these platforms sell doesn't even have to keep winning anyone over. Your attention gets bought, and the more reliably it gets bought, the more it's worth, and the more it's worth the harder the whole apparatus leans on capturing more of it. It feeds itself. There's no counterweight built in anywhere, no point where the machine has a reason to want less of you. It's on you to detach this parasite that is the algorithm.

By the way, the reason why I don't lump something like WhatsApp in with all this is that it's a **communication** platform (the unfortunate part is that it still harnesses your data and sells it accordingly, but that's another topic). Snapchat is the interesting middle case, at least according to the last time I used it (middle school, so 8 years back). People were constantly messaging on it, but they weren't hooked the way they're hooked on a feed, even when "media" components were starting to be added.

It's very telling if you look where the app's gravity points you. WhatsApp opens to a conversation with a person. Snapchat opens to a camera. The feed opens to a wall of strangers. Whatever it puts in front of you first is what it's actually for, and that first screen quietly tells you whether you're holding a communication tool or an attention machine in a friendly coat. WhatsApp and Snapchat point you at a person or moment. The feed points you at strangers. That's the whole difference.


## Why I Can't Just Look Away

Fine. It's easy to accept that it's designed to catch me. The harder, more uncomfortable question is why I can't just avoid it once I know all this.

In my opinion, a lot of the usual hooks don't really land on me (because I've thought about and dealt with them). The outrage, the FOMO, the anxiety of being left out. I think I'm fairly fortified against that stuff (or maybe I just use the app less, so I'm less exposed). But once I sat with it, I noticed those hooks all share something: they're the kind you *can* build a defense against. They work by getting you to care about something, and caring is a judgment. You can talk yourself out of a judgment. That's what mental fortitude is: seeing something and keeping your state firm.

Then there's the other kind of capture, and this one no amount of fortitude will protect you. If something is funny to you, you will laugh. You don't get to decide otherwise. Everyone can be entertained one way or another, and when you consider the sheer mass of content on these platforms, there's always going to be something for someone.

The first kind of capture runs on a judgment, and you can argue with a judgment. The second runs on a reflex, and you cannot argue with a reflex. The swipe scroll itself is on the reflex side too. The plain act of flicking from one video to the next is enough to hold you regardless of what the content even is. It's not something you're reasoning your way into.

You can see it in how people watch, too. More than half of users will skip a video the second it runs past a minute, even when it's on something they're interested in. That's not just patience failing. It's the reflex triggering. The next thing is always one thumb-flick away. That is the actual trap. It was never any single video. It's the loop. You get a piece of content you didn't choose, and the next one costs you nothing to reach, so the moment where you'd decide to stop never actually arrives. The "put it down" decision needs a gap to take place, but the design of these platforms prevents that gap from existing.


## Concluding remarks

Social media became entertainment. That's part of what I was trying to say throughout this piece. The numbers corroborate it, and my own experience affirms it. The people making the content are jesters, performing for the masses, recording themselves doing stuff and hoping we engage. The platform is the venue they perform in. And we're the ones at the tables, swiping.

I keep wanting to say that's harmless. It's just people laughing at funny videos. If only it were as simple as that. I'm not sure that's where my reasoning takes me, and that's part of what keeps this topic top of mind for me. If the venue turns out to be harmful, then the jesters aren't clean either. You don't get to participate in something harmful and wave it off as not your doing. It boils down to: does entertainment itself carries any genuine value (as referenced from the previous piece), or does it just feel that way?
