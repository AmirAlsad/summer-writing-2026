When you start to look at questions as challenges, everything changes.

In my observations of other people and reflections on society, I've come to realize that most people doesn't want to ask questions. To them, its friction.

It'll be easier to explain if I draw it out.

This is what the whole thought process looks like:
![](friction-1.jpg)

The jagged region is friction-ful:
![](friction-2.jpg)

Pushing the box represents the mental effort and the jagged region represents a question. Pushing the box over the jagged, friction-ful region is very difficult compared to the ease of pushing it over the surrounding friction-less surface.

Most people, when facing a question, tunnel-vision on the friction. They fail to zoom out and see the broader vision:
![](friction-3.jpg)

In that broader vision, it can be seen that getting through the question, id est finding an answer, is a means for getting over the wall. Getting over the wall itself still requires work, but an answer to the question was what made it possible. Now, this broader vision that I've drawn isn't appealing on it's own. Why would anyone want to get over the wall? Well, the idea is that the wall is an abstract representation of any barrier that separates us from what we want:
![](friction-4.jpg)

So, how does this tie to looking at questions as challenges? Well, a challenge is something difficult that has some positive benefit once completed. In a sense, looking at questions like challenges is equivalent to zooming out and seeing the broader vision.
