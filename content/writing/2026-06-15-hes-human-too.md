Friday was the SpaceX IPO. I knew it was happening that day, so I kept my laptop open and checked in every so often, waiting for it to actually hit the market. I didn't know the exact time, so I just refreshed here and there. In between, I read up on the mechanics of the thing, how the structure of it basically lets anyone get in (not literally everyone, and you can look into the matter yourself for more details).

Then it opened. I watched the number shoot up, watched everyone on X celebrating, and I felt something. It wasn't excitement. It was closer to worry. The same worry I get when I watch someone I care about make a decision that's obviously poor and not well thought out. Like if one of my siblings told me they were going through with something I knew wasn't good for them. That kind of feeling. It comes from a place of care. I'm probably not the best interpreter of my own feelings, but that's what it was.

To be clear, I don't hold a position here. I'm not betting against it and I'm not betting on it. I don't hold anything in the market at all, and I'm not a financial advisor.

Within a couple of days it was worth more than Tesla and, as I write this, closing in on Amazon. A two-and-a-half trillion dollar company, all while losing billions of dollars a year.

After a lot of thinking, I think I'll make a big claim: people don't actually want what SpaceX is selling.

And by people I mean people generally. Not investors, not businesses, not the niche crowd that gets excited about this stuff. Normal people.

Traveling to Mars is a cool idea. I'll give it that. But it isn't something a normal person actually prioritizes. Nobody's lying awake wanting it. At best the want is conditional, "sure, if it makes life on Earth better or more convenient, go for it."

When I look at the most impactful innovations, it's something that people already wanted but done **better.** Whether better refers to performance or experience depends on the product, but the core idea is that the demand must initially be there, even if it's a bit obscure. Take a look at the iPhone for reference. People already wanted to talk to each other, to play, to find their way around. What Apple did was notice that the technology sat awkwardly between you and the thing you already wanted, and they removed that awkwardness. Mars isn't that. There's no want already sitting underneath it, waiting to be unlocked.

The closest thing to a real want buried in all this is compute, the whole orbital data center idea, and even that's indirect. People don't care about compute. They care about what compute gives them, the AI that helps them think and work. And even there, I suspect a lot of the demand is more inflated than real. Whether you can even run a data center in space is its own question to be asked, though I'll honestly put it away for more credible people to tackle.

The worst part about all of it, and the matter that I sat down to write about, is how much of this rests on one man.

Elon has done remarkable things. I won't pretend otherwise. But somewhere along the way, people started holding him above human comparison, as if the ordinary rules stopped applying to him. And in all reality, he's just another human. He eats. He drinks. He sleeps. He gets older. He has relationships, and memories, and a body that will function as every body does. It frightens me how much value we've decided to attribute to one person like that.

If you ask me why the market keeps buying it, why the number keeps climbing, I don't have a clever answer. I think it's pure narrative gravity. A good enough story pulls everything toward it. I don't think there's anything more reasonable than that going on here, at least not that I can see.

I've got actual reasons behind all these claims, but I'm not in a position to lay them all out right now. I'll save it all for the next piece. 

Until next time, ✌️.
