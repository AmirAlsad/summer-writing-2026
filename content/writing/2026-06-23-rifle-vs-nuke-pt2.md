Last piece I laid the framework out, but there were lots of ideas that I didn't include. Some of those examples ended up being remarkable useful. So, this piece is the messier version. Diving into a few of the examples that shaped the thing.


## The Landmine

A landmine goes off on whoever stumbles into it. Indiscriminate. In that sense, when compared to the rifle and nuke, it's leaning more towards the nuke side of things.

But that's not right. A landmine still pretty much affects one person, albeit a bit more brutally. And if you think about how landmines actually get used (a marked minefield in a war zone), they mostly don't end up hitting normal people (not that I would know much about the topic, but I would suppose this is the case).

And so, there's two things that this matter breaks into. One is how many people a single use hurts. The other is whether the harm lands on the people actually in the fight, or spills onto bystanders who never signed up for it. A landmine is small on the first, and on the second it's not nearly as indiscriminate. A nuke happens to be maximal on both. The landmine pried this categorization into the finer two.

There's another weird thing about the landmine. It keeps going after you've stopped deciding to use it. A rifle stops when you stop pulling the trigger; a landmine sits there for years and kills someone long after the war's over. That's really just a flavor of not being able to take it back, which is the same recoverability idea from last piece.

At a deeper level, the landmine brought out a key question: do you judge a technology by how it's *usually* used, or by its *worst*? For whether it hits bystanders, the question is whether you can use it without hitting them at all. For how bad it gets, you judge the worst.


## The Truck

Last piece I said a thing gets turned into a weapon one of two ways: either someone builds it for that on purpose, or someone repurposes whatever's already lying around. The best example is pretty epic: a truck with a machine gun strapped on top.

Disregarding the machine gun for a second, strapping it onto a truck is a very simple repurposing, and it dramatically increases the capacity of the thing. The truck didn't make the gun deadlier per shot. It made it mobile and fast. So, repurposing isn't really about *creating* a weapon. It's about delivering and amplifying one that already exists.

That distinction is essential for understanding how far a thing spreads. A purpose-built weapon has a clear supply gate (you have to actually manufacture it). A repurposed everyday object rides on how common the everyday object already is, and trucks are everywhere. Ties back to the idea that anything that can be used as a weapon, assuming the weapon would actually be effective, will eventually get built or repurposed into one.


## DNA

We can also look to the history. I looked at the recombinant DNA scare of the 1970s.

In the early '70s, biologists worked out how to splice genes from one organism into another. Huge upside (it's the base of basically all modern biotech), but some fear came out of it. A lot of the work used E. coli, the bacterium that lives in your gut, and people worried someone would *accidentally* build a strain that was antibiotic-resistant, or cancer-causing, or toxic, and that it would get out of the lab and into people who had no defense against it.

This was all from the scientists themselves. In 1974, a bunch of the field's biggest names published a letter, all at once, calling for a voluntary halt on the most dangerous experiments until they could figure out how to run them safely. A field hitting pause on itself. Sound familiar? Then, in early 1975, a chunk of them met at a conference center in California (the Asilomar conference) to decide what to actually do.

Their conclusion wasn't to ban it, but it wasn't a general "go ahead" either. They sorted the research into tiers by how dangerous it was, and matched each tier to a level of containment. Harmless stuff got handled lightly. Scary stuff got serious containment. The most dangerous experiments of all were simply forbidden, and they left some room to tighten or loosen the rules as they learned more.

Applying it to the framework that we've been constructing: Gene splicing clears the offense-capable gate, but most of it is recoverable. Though, at the extreme, it's an irreversible catastrophy and spills onto everyone. We can't use the framework to cleanly classify gene splicing, because there's different levels of danger. So, in a more abstract sense, the framework shouldn't hand back a single answer. It should be able to provide a range of possibilities. It shouldn't be about classifying the technology, but about classifying the use cases. In cleaner examples where the technology is designed for an offensive use case (like rifles), then we don't have to worry about this abstraction. If we abstracted nukes into nuclear technology, then we'd have to separate nuclear energy from nuclear bombs. We can contain the simpler items and get more aggressive with the use cases that near towards nuke tier. Basically, we're copying what the biologists improvised in 1975.

The plague never came from the labs (unless we're counting COVID-19, but that's another discussion). And the technology has transformed into the bio-tech industry.


## Software as a Munition

Let's finally get to the software considerations. As I write this, a government is export-controlling an AI model like it's a missile. So, I went looking for other times software got treated as a munition, to see how those went and see the patterns that history paints. There are at least three worth knowing about, and we'll be running them through the framework to try and understand them.

The first is the crypto wars of the 1990s, when the US classified strong encryption as a munition. In all honesty, when I first head about this, I assumed "crypto wars" was going to be about something like cryptocurrency or hacking. It's not. It's about encryption, which is a *defensive* thing. You can't point it at anyone and hurt them. So, it never even clears the offense-capable gate, which is partially why the whole "offense-capable" requirement came about. The whole story is pretty cool, but it'll have to wait for another time. Long story short, the government restrictions failed and encryption became accessible to anyone that needed it (as you may know).

The second case is 3D-printed guns, which sorta sits between software and hardware. In 2013, a guy named Cody Wilson posted the files for "The Liberator," a printable plastic pistol, and the design got downloaded a hundred thousand times in two days. The State Department came down on him under the same arms-export law, arguing that putting the blueprints online counted as exporting a weapon. He sued, lost in the lower courts, and the government eventually settled. Notice that the government itself eventually conceded the files don't confer any real military advantage, which is a fancy way of saying that it's rifle-class. It's a weapon, sure. But it's a recoverable, one-target, rifle-class one. The lock-down fell apart the way the framework says a rifle's should. Obviously, this isn't the same case in other nations where rifle-class weaponry is prohibited from the public.

The third case is the closest to what's happening with AI right now (pure software). It's the fight over "intrusion software," which is basically hacking tools and the exploits that power them. After the Arab Spring, it came out that Western companies had been selling spyware to regimes that used it to hunt dissidents, so in 2013, a big group of countries (the Wassenaar Arrangement, if you want to look it up) added these tools to their export-control lists. When the US tried to actually enforce it, the security world revolted, for a reason that should sound very familiar. The exact same tools defenders use to find and fix holes are the tools attackers use to break in. Lock them down and you limit the defense as much as the offense. The rule got walked back and narrowed. Offense-capable, but dual-use, sitting right in that third bucket/classification.

These three cases practically look the same from a distance: software gets called a weapon, the government tries to bottle it, the lock-down struggles and eventually fails. But, the framework says they struggled for three completely different reasons. Crypto was a defensive tool, not an offense-capable one. The gun files were a real weapon, but rifle-class, so the controls were overkill. The intrusion software was a real weapon but also dual-use, so controlling it choked the defenders, which is why it got narrowed.

What's really interesting about the third case is that you can swap "intrusion software" for "AI model" and you've basically got today's headline.
